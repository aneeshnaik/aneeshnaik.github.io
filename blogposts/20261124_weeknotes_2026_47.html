Species and Habitat Mapping

I had a good couple of weeks on the species mapping and habitat mapping front. To summarise:

  • Having previously claimed this a few times, I now truly have done my last species mapping experiments with the GeoPlant dataset. What I’ve come out with from this exercise is an understanding of how best to do species distribution modelling with Tessera.1 I’ve put all my figures together into a manuscript, and I’m working on writing this up.
  • I’m now turning this pipeline to the UK, building plant species maps using various datasets. One advance over my GeoPlant pipeline: I’m incorporating it into a Bayesian workflow, so that we get (hopefully well-calibrated) uncertainties on our species maps. I spent an extremely enjoyable day working out the theoretical framework for this.
  • With a quick first pass at the pipeline, I’ve made species maps of 124 plant species across Great Britain. Here are some examples:

1 In particular, I’ve built a pipeline for plant SDMs with Tessera that would have won the 2024 GeoLifeClef competition.

  • With these species maps in hand, I then have a plant assemblage at each pixel. Using some labelled habitat polygons, a model can then learn an assemblage-habitat link, and make a habitat map. I have access to a large number of such habitat polygons: the ground-truth polygons used to create the Living England habitat maps. Using these polygons, I made two habitat maps: one using this species-up approach, and one with the conventional top-down approach (i.e., simply training a habitat classifier with remote sensing covariates). The figure below compares the two maps.
  • The agreement between the two maps is rather good, despite the differing methodologies / philosophies.2 This is encouraging, because I much prefer this (unconventional) species-up approach for two reasons. First, it aligns better with a more ecological understanding of habitat. Second, with this kind of approach, one is able to map a little more fuzzily, i.e., look at how species assemblages vary spatially and use this to map more spatially gradual boundaries between habitats.

2 A few months ago I wrote a little about the Clementsian versus Gleasonian philosophies regarding ecological communities. We can understand these top-down and species-up approaches to habitat mapping as being approximately Clementsian and Gleasonian respectively.

I haven’t yet turned on the Bayesian switch. Will experiment with that next week!
